title: "Gabriel's Horn is a Lesson in Mathematical Modeling"
---

Gabriel's Horn is a classic lesson in first-year integral
calculus. If you need a refresher, Gabriel's Horn is the
surface obtained by revolving the graph of $y=\frac{1}{x}$,
$x\geq 1$ around the $x$-axis. It's famous for having
*infinite* surface area, while also having *finite* volume.

The strangeness of Gabriel's Horn is often expressed through
the *Painter's paradox*. If Gabriel's Horn has finite
volume, that means you could pour a finite amount of paint
into it. However, since it has infinite surface area, you'd
need infinite paint to cover the surface!

A typical lesson on Gabriel's Horn might proceed as follows:
- The instructor defines the surface of revolution
- Then, they compute the volume and surface area
- Finally, they mention the Painter's paradox and make a
  comment about how counterintuitive math can be.

This is a perfectly good lesson to show the mechanics of
finding surface area and volumes of revolution, however I
think the typical presentation misses a great opportunity to
get the students thinking about the role of mathematics as
a modeling tool in the applied sciences and engineering.

As a Master's student, I taught this lesson in a tutorial
for *Calculus II* at McGill, to a class of mostly
    engineering students. It was just off of lockdown, this
    tutorial was these students only in-person instruction,
    and I had built a good rapport with them over the term.
    After I presented the typical Gabriel's Horn lecture, I
    was asked "What does this *mean* physically?" and I
    replied "It means your mathematical model is wrong --
    have you ever seen an infinitely long object?"

The following inpromptu discussion was good, but ever since
then I've thought about fleshing out Gabriel's Horn into a
lesson on mathematical modeling. Let me try to do that now.

I'd begin in the usual fashion, introducing the surface and
computing its volume and surface area. Then I'd describe the
Painter's paradox. Next I would ask the students "What does
this mathematical result tell us about the real-world?"
